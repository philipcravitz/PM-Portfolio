Fourteen months into a job search changes the way you look at hiring.
At first, rejection feels personal. Then it becomes statistical.

I started looking at the hiring process the way I would look at any other product.
- What is the system optimizing for?
- What signals is it using?
- Which users are being filtered out?
- And perhaps most importantly, what false negatives is it creating?
###
### The Noise vs. The Craft
The market today rewards noise. AI is everywhere. Transformation is everywhere. Every résumé is becoming a carefully optimized collection of keywords.

But there is still a difference between knowing how to describe a capability and actually knowing how to solve a problem.

When you refuse to sell snake oil or overpromise your qualifications, the path is harder. The rejections pile up. But eventually, every "no" stops being a verdict on your worth and becomes a data point. You start asking a different question: What can I learn from this?

When Every Opportunity Is a Forced March
In an extended search, one does not always have the luxury of holding out for the "ideal" alignment. Every pursuit becomes a forced march. You take on complex, adjacent challenges. You dive headfirst into unfamiliar tech stacks, unglamorous operational bottlenecks, or domains outside your primary background.

And here is the secret: saying "yes" to what isn't a perfect match isn't a compromise. It is an engine for growth.

When necessity forces you to bridge a domain gap, you have to relearn how to learn. You strip away corporate polish and rely on raw execution, foundational problem-solving, and sheer grit.

You discover that a seasoned product mind doesn't need five years in a hyper-specific vertical to figure out where the funnel is leaking, why users drop off, or how to build systems that scale. Domain expertise is great to have, but fundamental knowledge that transfers between disciplines can be applied with veteran speed, wisdom, and judgment. These aren't just acts of knowledge. They are acts of character. 

### The Advantage of Being Tested
A long search strips away fluff. It leaves behind an operator who is battle-tested, deeply pragmatic, and entirely unbothered by comfort zones.
A candidate who matches 100% of a static checklist may be the safest choice on paper. But the ability to navigate the unknown rarely appears in a checklist.

The best product minds aren't defined by the exact software they used yesterday, but by how quickly they can master the challenges of tomorrow.
A résumé can prove where you've been. It can't fully prove how quickly you can learn where you're going.

Fourteen months of rejection has taught me that the ability to navigate the unknown is its own kind of experience.

And when the right opportunity finally arrives, I won't be arriving with a perfect résumé. I'll be arriving ready to work.
