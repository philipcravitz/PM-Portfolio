---
title: "Rent the Plumbing, Build the House"
date: "December 9, 2025"

Some product debates refuse to die. Build or buy is the kitchen ghost of tech. You turn the lights on and it's sitting there again, staring at you like you owe it money. Everyone has an opinion, and half of them are based on vibes instead of math. So here is the thing: build vs buy is not a morality play. It is resource allocation dressed up like a philosophy argument.

Start with the question: Is this thing a commodity or a differentiator? Common core functions like payment processing, chat widgets, login auth, or anything that exists purely to not break are almost never worth building. Customers do not wake up hoping you hand coded a bespoke credit card flow. They just want their card to work and for your product to not set their bank account on fire. That is plumbing. You rent plumbing.

Your differentiators are where you build. Your proprietary algorithm. Your specialized workflow. Your weird, wonderful way of solving the problem that your competitors cannot easily mimic. That stuff is your advantage. You keep it close. You do not outsource your identity.

Another layer to this puzzle is customization. When you build, you tailor the thing to your exact needs. When you buy, you may still have to customize, and the real question becomes how far that customization has to go. If the effort to bend a vendor tool into shape grows larger than the opportunity cost of building your own, the math starts pointing in a different direction. Buying is only efficient when the customization stays lightweight.

You also have the hidden costs that keep CFOs awake. Building is not Dev Salary x Hours. Building is Dev Salary + Maintenance For Eternity + the Opportunity Cost of everything your best people are not doing. Every time you ask a senior engineer to fix a homegrown internal tool, you are paying them to be a mechanic instead of an engineer designing the next big product innovation. Your internal tool is not the product. Your product is the product.

Buying, though, is a little time machine. It gets you to validation faster than your roadmap ever will. You can spend half a year engineering something from scratch or you can buy the off the shelf version and learn what the market actually wants in a few months. If you have the luxury of budget and integration resources, I have become fond of the idea of Buy to Learn and Build to Scale. Buy when you need speed. Build when you have proof. If you outgrow the vendor two years later, congratulations. You have a scaling problem instead of a guessing problem.

Great product management is not a contest to see who can hire the biggest engineering team. It is the art of placing your limited chips on the bets that create real value. Outsource the distractions. Invest in the crown jewels. Point your smartest people at the work that moves the business forward instead of the work that merely keeps the lights on.
