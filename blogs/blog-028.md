AI Cyrano de Bergerac can whisper “perfect” lines into a recruiter’s ear, but when the stage lights hit, will you own every word?

It's true. If you don't use AI to align your resume with a job description, you're bringing a knife to a gunfight. The standard advice is simple: Feed your history into the maw of an LLM, let it spit out a version of you that is calibrated for an Applicant Tracking System (ATS), and pray that the algorithm likes what it sees.

But if I let a machine hallucinate my accomplishments, I’m not just cheating the system; I’m poisoning the well of my own reputation.

In Product Management, we talk about fiduciary duty: the legal and moral obligation to act in the best interest of another. I believe this duty doesn't start on your first day of work. It starts with your resume. If I start a professional relationship based on a "trick," I have already compromised my ability to lead with integrity.

### The Bolding Audit: AI as an Editor, Not a Ghostwriter
I do use AI. But I use it as a rigorous, high-friction editor, not a magic wand. My process is designed to be intentionally laborious because integrity requires effort.
First, I provide the AI with a "Source of Truth" reference document: a hard-coded list of my actual wins, metrics, and skills. The AI is allowed to rearrange the furniture, but it isn’t allowed to build new rooms.

Then comes the Bolding Audit.

When the AI suggests a "customized" version of a bullet point, I bold every single word it changed or added. This creates a visual "delta" between my original truth and the AI’s optimization. I read every line. If the AI suggests I "spearheaded a 20% growth initiative" when I actually "supported a 15% improvement," I don't just delete the lie; I question why the machine felt the need to embellish.

If a word is bolded, I must be able to defend it in the harsh light of an interview. If I can’t stand behind it with absolute accuracy, it doesn't make the cut. This is my personal Daylight Test: If I had to explain my "optimization" process to a hiring manager, would they feel respected or played?

### Solidarity with the "Intelligent Adult"
The irony is that "gaming the system" works in the short term. You can trick an algorithm into giving you an interview. But short-term strategy only requires opportunity; long-term strategy requires trust.

By refusing to be a digital deepfake, I am performing a strategic filter.

A company that rewards someone for gaming the ATS is likely a company that will reward you for gaming the market and its users once you’re on the inside. They are looking for shortcut-seekers. I am looking for solidarity: for a place that values fiduciary focus and honest utility.

I treat the hiring manager like the Intelligent Adult they are. I know they are being flooded with AI-generated sludge: generic, flowery bullet points that sound impressive but mean nothing. By presenting a resume that has been refined by tech but authored by human conviction, I am signaling that I build for the marathon, not the facade.

### The High Price of Integrity
Does this make me a "bad" candidate in a hyper-competitive market? Perhaps to some. It is certainly slower. It requires more mental calories. It means I apply to fewer jobs but with higher intentionality.

But in a world where products (and people) fight over microscopic molecules of trust, my real asset is my reputation. I would rather be a human with a clear conscience than a perfectly optimized keyword-mirror.

Trust compounds longer than any "hacked" resume ever will. If a company doesn't want the person who audits their own bolded lines, they probably don't want the kind of Product Manager I am anyway.

I’d rather show up to the interview with my own 'large nose' and a defensible truth than be the person who gets hired for a soul that belongs to an algorithm. In the end, the stage lights always reveal the truth. Make sure you’re ready to own every line.
