Who's afraid of the big bad wolf? PM's shouldn't be. You should huff, puff, and blow your own ideas down before the market does it for you.

Imagine the classic Wolf of fairy tales as the sudden market shift, the budget cut, or the stakeholder scrutiny that tests whether your roadmap is actually built to last. As a leader, your job is to play the role of the Wolf. Not to destroy ideas, but to stress-test them.

### The Fallacy of the Straw Man
The fastest way to "win" an argument is to build a Straw Man. You take an engineer's concern about technical debt or a designer's push for a better UX, and you strip it of its nuance. You frame it as "over-engineering" or "gold-plating" so you can easily knock it down and keep moving.

But you didn't actually defeat the idea; you defeated a caricature of it. The "Straw Man" wasn't there until you created it to justify a shortcut. This is "Command and Control" at its most fragile, and it leaves the team, and the product, vulnerable to the real wolf.

### The Steel-Man Standard: Propping Up the House
True stewardship requires the Steel-man approach. Instead of misrepresenting an idea to destroy it, you work to prop it up. You give the idea a fighting chance to be right.

Before you test a teammate's proposal, you help them strengthen it:
- Add the missing support: "To make this work within our current architecture, we’d need to solve for X first. Let's assume we can."

- Clarify the upside: "If this succeeds, it doesn't just fix a bug; it reduces our support load by 20%. Let's lead with that."

- Build the frame: You help them articulate the strongest possible version of their argument before you apply the pressure.

### The Test: Blowing the House Down
Once the idea is propped up with steel, then, and only then, do you huff and puff. You apply the hard questions of the Iron Triangle:

Does this structure survive the deadline? Can the budget sustain this level of rigor? Does this hold up against our primary objective for the quarter?

If the house stands, you’ve found a resilient, steel-reinforced solution. If it falls, it was tested meaningfully. The author of the idea doesn't feel dismissed by a fallacy; they feel included in a rigorous process of discovery. They saw the "Wolf" coming, and they saw exactly where the structure failed.

### The Result: Shared Conviction
Product Management is the art of ordering evidence under the weight of real-world constraints. By acting as the "Internal Wolf," you ensure that the "External Wolf" (the market) has nothing left to blow down.

When you help your team forge their ideas with steel before testing them, you aren't just making a roadmap. You are building a shared conviction that can weather any storm.

Drive toward the deadline. Just make sure the ideas that get there are made of steel, not straw.
