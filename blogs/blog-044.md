In product design, there are two easy answers. If a product is simple and transactional, a straight, fast highway to self-service is built. If the product is complex, like lending or specialized healthcare; a high-touch bridge to an expert who can navigate the fog is required.

But the "Easy Answers" don't account for the messy reality of the middle ground. A great Product Manager doesn't just pick a side; they deconstruct the layers of the journey of the user:

- **The Complexity Layer**: Identifying where a transaction becomes a transformation. In lending, a user might start with a simple search but end with a need for a deep-dive analysis.

- **The Generational Layer**: Accounting for the digital-native who wants to solve it alone and the traditionalist who won't move forward without a voice on the line.

- **The Operational Layer**: Balancing the need of the company for low-cost, digital-first operations with the need of the customer for confidence.
###
The mistake is forcing a single path. Driving everyone to self-service can create something most dashboards fail to capture: completion anxiety. Conversely, forcing a high-touch interaction on a simple task is just expensive friction.

The best digital experiences aren’t rigid funnels. They are liquid paths: systems that adapt to hesitation, confidence, complexity, and intent in real time. Let digital-first users move independently with confidence-building feedback loops, progress indicators, and automated accuracy checks. Let them flow down the digital path until they hit a wall, or a realization that they need a guide. Nobody wants to feel trapped halfway through a mortgage application at 11:30 PM and giving them a way out or way forward could save the transaction and the customer.

For those who want to talk to someone, create frictionless entry points. But even then, arm them with confidence-building self-service tools during the wait. Sometimes the best "outcome path" isn't the one the user prefers initially; it’s the one that ensures it is actually finished.

As PMs, one is not just building buttons; it is about building the machine that guides the user through hesitation. We separate the layers, grease the transitions, and ensure that whether the user is clicking or talking, they are moving toward success.

Because at the end of the day, a low-cost operation is a failure if the customer walks away frustrated. Design is not just for preference and aesthetics; it is to ensure the "best" possible outcome.
