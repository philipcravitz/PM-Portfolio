We love to fall in love. We love our ideas. They often arrive as inspiration with singing choirs and halos of perfection; on paper, they are flawless. Then, as Product Managers, we lift those ideas into reality.

Early in development, the most crucial step is testing those ideas against the market. But when do we know it’s time to kill our darlings, and when do we keep building scaffolding to help the idea sing?

In early product work, you’re not building a cathedral. You’re stress-testing an idea that barely exists. That’s why we use low-fidelity mocks, prototypes, and qualitative testing. We want truth while it’s still cheap.

But there’s a moment every team hits: Feedback comes back cold. Confused. Indifferent. Now you’re stuck with the hardest decision in product management: Is this a bad idea, or just bad execution?

### Bad Ideas Need a Mercy Kill
If users don’t care about the value in its rawest form, you don’t have a UX problem. You have a concept problem. No UI polish or roadmap optimism will fix that. This is where a "Founder Mindset" can hurt you, it convinces you to push forward when you should step back.
Killing a weak concept early is not failure. It’s stewardship. It protects time, focus, and credibility for the ideas that actually deserve iteration.

### Good Idea, Bad Execution
Sometimes the signal is real, but the delivery is broken. Users want the solution; they just can’t navigate it yet. You don’t scrap the engine here, you tune it. You simplify and rework until intent becomes usability.

The danger is confusing these two failures with each other. Too many teams spend months optimizing something nobody actually wanted. That’s not persistence; that’s denial with better branding.

### The Soft Skill of Scrapping it All 
Real product leadership isn’t just about building. It’s about knowing when to burn the blueprint before the system starts to lie to you. This requires a rare mix of low-ego and high-intelligence. It’s the ability to see the data, feel the market, and make the hard choice that is right for the business. You can’t put that on a resume, but it shows up in your work every time you put your mind to a task.
