In the rush to automate everything with AI, we're forgetting the most important part of the product isn't the output: it’s the person responsible for it.

We've spent years obsessed with "frictionless" design. The goal is to make the product a slide: one push and the user ends up at the bottom. Great success!

With AI, it's tempting to double down. We want the tool to guess the intent, generate the output, and ship the result. But there is a quiet risk in letting the product take the entire journey: the person in the center can eventually lose their connection to the outcome.

Rather than aiming for total automation, we might consider the value of Strategic Incompleteness. This is the practice of designing systems that refuse to fully close the loop, not because they cannot, but because they should not. By intentionally stopping just short of completion, the product creates a moment of pause where human judgment, accountability, and ownership can step back into the process.

AI is a world-class assistant for repetitive, low-stakes "grunt work." It is excellent at prepping the ingredients of a recipe. However, as the task moves into critical thinking, we might think of the AI less as a "Doer" and more as an "Advisor."

Even if the AI’s assessment is highly accurate, there is a case for keeping the person in the loop. Accountability is difficult to outsource. If the AI handles the entire process, we risk losing the "tasting" skills that high-stakes work requires.

Many users simply want the best answer so they can move on with their day. The challenge for a Product Manager is to show the work without overwhelming the person. We can think of this as Layered Transparency:

The Value-Add Summary: For the user who wants the "best" choice, provide the recommendation clearly, but frame it as a well-supported suggestion.

The "Show Your Work" Option: For the user who needs to verify, provide a collapsible "Logic" section. Show the evidence and the alternatives, but keep them tucked away so they don't clutter the experience.

The 90/10 Draft: In many cases, providing a 90% complete solution is more empowering than a 100% finished one. It allows the person to add the final, human touch that turns a generic output into something they truly own.

If a product makes people feel like they are just "rubber-stamping" a machine’s work, they may eventually feel disconnected from the craft. But if the product handles the menial scaffolding and invites the person back in for the critical "bricks," the tool becomes an extension of their own capability.

The goal isn't to build a system that works in spite of the person. It is to build a system that thrives because of them. By designing for incompleteness, we aren't creating more work; we are protecting the human agency that makes the work meaningful.
