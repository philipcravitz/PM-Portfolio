Bureaucracy isn’t built by villains. It’s built by heroes working in isolation.

The Security team creates a checklist to prevent a breach. The Legal team builds a review gate to mitigate risk. Engineering designs an intake form to protect their focus. In a vacuum, every one of these "Best Practices" is a win: they are smart, logical, and defensible.

But when you put them all together without a central architect, or fail to consider the totality of the process, you get a traffic-jammed highway of requests and ideas, honking their horns trying to get onto the next scheduled call, review, or toll-gate meeting.

Most people see this red tape as a series of obstacles to be climbed over. The process is like weather: something to be endured or complained about. But Product Managers use this process as the lifeblood of creation and execution. To accept it as-is is to ignore the opportunity to treat the process as a product itself.

The mistake we make is overspecializing our "checks" while ignoring our "flow." We spend a decade perfecting the silos, but we neglect the connective tissue.

A great PM doesn't just cut red tape; they deconstruct it. They look at the end-to-end system and realize that speed isn’t about working harder: it’s about smoothing the transitions, finding the synergies, and cutting the redundancies. A "quality gate" that nobody understands is actually just a wall. A review in isolation is an inefficiency just waiting to be optimized.
I’ve spent my career in the guts of these systems, from startups to legacy giants like Wells Fargo. The breakthrough never comes from adding more "process." It comes from the hard work of aligning those isolated good intentions into a single, complementary stream.

A PM’s skill isn't just knowing how to build a feature; it’s knowing how to build the machine that builds the feature. While we are typically called upon to shepherd a product to market and watch it dazzle in the light of production, the real work happens in the dark. We need to get our hands dirty in the machinery, greasing the process that pushes us toward that finished output.

That dirty job is as critical as the finished product. It tools the factory. It sets the quality controls. It allows other teams to provide the valuable input they need to provide. We shouldn't be fighting it, avoiding it, or rolling our eyes in frustration. We should be working the problem like any other product, making the internal production of our own factory floor hum like any other well-produced output.

As PMs, we have serious skin in this game. We are the ones who look at the tangled mess of convolution and see the speedway hidden underneath. We just have to raise our hand and propose the better path.

At the end of the day, a polished UI doesn't matter if the system behind it is grinding to a halt.
