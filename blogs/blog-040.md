The biggest mistake isn’t making the wrong call. It’s not learning from it when you do.

I've shipped a design that cut contact rates nearly in half overnight.

I've pitched a CMS integration three separate times, with data, with timing arguments, with a four-month estimate nobody wanted to hear, and lost every single round.

I'm not telling you this because the story gets better. I'm telling you because those two failures taught me more than most of my wins ever did.

The contact module failure was fast and visible. We were moving quickly through a large digital redesign. I pushed the team toward an accordion tile that defaulted to show a short form and a phone number. My reasoning felt solid. It was based on patterns I thought I understood. The team was cooperative, energized, and nobody pumped the brakes.

That should have been my first warning sign.

When everyone agrees too easily, it usually means the culture in the room is optimized for speed, not scrutiny. I had led that culture. So when contacts dropped from 4% to 2.5% after launch, I couldn't point at the design alone. I had to point at the moment I felt a pang of doubt and kept moving anyway. That pang is data. I don't ignore it anymore.

The CMS failure was slower and quieter, which made it harder to learn from.

I saw the need for flexible, segmented lead forms before the demand materialized. I pitched it early and lost. I pitched it again with supporting data and lost. I showed leadership a four-month build estimate when the first new form request came in. Still lost. The vision was right. The story wasn't landing.

What I eventually understood: anticipating growth is an art, not a formula. Sometimes you build the capability and never need it. Sometimes you don't build it and spend years wishing you had. The bet itself isn't always wrong. But if you can't tell the story in a way that moves people at the right moment, the bet dies in the pitch room regardless.

Scar tissue isn't failure residue. It's pattern recognition you cannot buy.

The accordion module taught me to protect doubt. To make space for the person in the room who isn't sure yet, because that hesitation might be the most important signal on the table.

The CMS pitches taught me that vision without narrative is just a memo nobody reads twice.

I still get it wrong on both sides. Build something that never gets used. Miss the window on something that would have mattered. But I get it wrong differently now, with more awareness of which mistake I'm making and why.

That's what experience actually is. Not a highlight reel. A scar map.
